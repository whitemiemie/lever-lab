    Your records

    Spot it outside the lab

    • Clicker-training a dog to roll over: shaping, with the click as the "food is coming" signal.
    • Slot machines pay on a variable ratio. Any pull might win, so people keep pulling.
    • Checking your phone for messages: a variable interval. They can arrive any time.
    • Cramming before a fixed exam date: a fixed-interval scallop. Slow at first, frantic at the end.
    • A lift button that won't light up: you jab it harder and faster before you give up. An extinction burst.

    Common mix-ups

    • Negative reinforcement isn't punishment. It makes a behaviour more likely by taking away something unpleasant.
    • Punishment alone doesn't teach what to do instead. Reinforcing the right behaviour works better.
    • Partial rewards make habits stubborn. Behaviour rewarded only sometimes is the hardest to extinguish.

    Four kinds of consequence

    Food is just one kind. A consequence either adds or removes something, and the behaviour then goes up or down.

    How to play

    Lever Lab has three short experiments. Your lab partner talks you through each one, and you can jump between them any time with the 1 · 2 · 3 buttons at the top. Been here before? The start screen lets you skip the tour.

    1. 1 · Shape. Biscuit the rat is hungry and has never touched a lever. Tap Feed and a pellet drops with a click. Whatever Biscuit was doing at that click becomes more likely. Reward small steps toward the lever (turn, walk over, rear up, press), one at a time.
    2. Timing is everything. Tap late and you reward whatever Biscuit is doing by then, even spinning in circles. You have 30 pellets.
    3. 2 · Schedules. Five trained rats, five rules for when food comes. Run them side by side and see whose pen climbs fastest.
    4. 3 · Extinction. Guess what happens when the feeder breaks, then find out.

    Keyboard: Space or F feeds. Esc closes this sheet.

    About the game

    Lever Lab is a playable version of the classic operant conditioning experiment. You train a simulated rat to press a lever, compare five reinforcement schedules, then watch extinction happen. Questions it helps you answer:

    • How can you teach an animal something it would never do by chance?
    • Why does the timing of a reward matter so much?
    • Why do some reward rules keep us going far longer than others?
    • What happens when rewards stop?

    The idea

    Operant conditioning is learning from consequences. A behaviour that is followed by something good tends to happen again; one that is followed by nothing, or by something bad, tends to fade. In classical conditioning an animal learns that one event predicts another. In operant conditioning it learns what its own actions lead to.

    Lever Lab lets you run the classic demonstration yourself: shape a rat to press a lever, compare reinforcement schedules, and watch extinction happen.

    Who was B. F. Skinner?

    Burrhus Frederic Skinner (1904–1990) was an American psychologist and a leading behaviourist: he wanted psychology to study only behaviour that can be observed and measured. As a young researcher at Harvard he built small chambers in which a rat could press a lever for food, and a recorder that drew every press as it happened. With these he showed how the consequences of an action change how often it happens, and he gave this kind of learning its name: operant conditioning.

    His work built on Edward Thorndike's law of effect and became one of the foundations of behaviour analysis. It is still used in animal training, classrooms and behaviour therapy.

    History

    1. 1898Edward Thorndike times cats escaping from puzzle boxes. Moves that led to escape got faster with practice. He later called this the law of effect.
    2. Early 1930sAt Harvard, B. F. Skinner (1904–1990) builds a small chamber where a rat can press a lever for food, and a recorder that draws every press.
    3. 1938The Behavior of Organisms sets out operant behaviour, reinforcement and extinction, measured as rate of response.
    4. 1943Waiting on wartime research in Minneapolis, Skinner and his students teach a pigeon to "bowl" by rewarding closer and closer swipes at a ball. He calls the method shaping.
    5. 1948"Superstition" in the pigeon: food delivered on a timer, whatever the bird does, leaves several birds repeating odd rituals. Later work questioned how much of this was learned (Staddon & Simmelhag, 1971).
    6. 1957Ferster and Skinner publish Schedules of Reinforcement, cataloguing the records that fixed and variable ratio and interval schedules produce.

    Skinner called the apparatus an operant chamber or lever box. "Skinner box" was a name other people gave it, and he did not like it.

    Key terms

    Operant
    A behaviour defined by what it does to the world, such as pressing a lever.
    Reinforcer
    A consequence that makes the behaviour before it more likely. Positive adds something (a pellet); negative removes something unpleasant.
    Punishment
    A consequence that makes the behaviour before it less likely.
    Primary and secondary reinforcers
    A primary reinforcer meets a basic need, like food for a hungry rat. A secondary (conditioned) reinforcer becomes rewarding by being paired with one, like the feeder's click, praise or tokens. The click lets a trainer mark the exact moment.
    Discriminative stimulus
    A cue that signals a response will now be reinforced, such as a light in the chamber that means "pressing works now".
    Shaping
    Reinforcing successive approximations: closer and closer versions of a target behaviour.
    Schedule of reinforcement
    The rule for which responses pay. Ratio schedules count responses; interval schedules count time. Either can be fixed or variable. Continuous reinforcement (CRF) pays every response.
    Partial reinforcement effect
    A response reinforced only some of the time is much harder to extinguish than one reinforced every time.
    Cumulative record
    A line that steps up with each response over time. Flat means no responding; steep means fast responding.
    Extinction
    Responding fades when it no longer pays. It often starts with an extinction burst: a short rise in responding.
    Spontaneous recovery
    After a rest, an extinguished response briefly comes back.
    Superstitious behaviour
    A behaviour strengthened by a reinforcer that happened to follow it by chance.
    Instinctive drift
    The pull of an animal's trained behaviour back toward its instinctive patterns. Not every animal can be taught anything (Breland & Breland, 1961).

    Why it matters

    Where it's used. Behaviour modification applies these ideas on purpose: token economies in classrooms and hospitals, time-out for children, applied behaviour analysis (ABA), which uses shaping to build skills step by step, and clicker training for pets and zoo animals. Biofeedback uses the same logic to help people gain control over body signals such as tension or heart rate.

    Common mix-ups.

    • Negative reinforcement is not punishment. Both kinds of reinforcement make a behaviour more likely; "negative" only means something unpleasant is taken away.
    • Punishment alone doesn't teach the right behaviour. It works best when it's immediate and consistent, and paired with reinforcing what you do want. Harsh punishment can bring fear, avoidance, lying or aggression.
    • Late or vague rewards backfire. As in part 1, a reward strengthens whatever happened just before it, wanted or not.

    Ethics. Variable-ratio rewards are powerful, which is why they show up in slot machines, loot boxes and endless social feeds. Knowing the pattern helps you notice when it is being used on you. Using conditioning on people should respect their consent and dignity, and real animal research must follow animal-welfare rules. No animals were used here.

    How the simulation works

    Biscuit is a small set of rules, not a recording of a real animal. She has a handful of habits (wandering, sniffing, grooming, spinning, turning to the lever, walking to it, rearing). Each has a weight, and she picks her next move in proportion to those weights.

    • When you tap Feed, the move she was making about 0.3 seconds earlier gets heavier (your reaction time is allowed for). Nearby steps toward the lever get a smaller boost.
    • A move that keeps happening without food drifts back toward its starting weight.
    • After 30 pellets she is full and stops working, as a well-fed animal would.
    • In parts 2 and 3 the press rate is set by the schedule and by time since the last pellet, following the patterns reported by Ferster and Skinner (1957). Those shapes are built in, not learned live, and the clock runs as a time-lapse.

    Real shaping takes longer. A real rat is first kept a little hungry and taught that the feeder's click means food (magazine training), and the numbers here are tuned so that a visit takes minutes, not hours.
